About this page

What it is
A map of who cites whom in law, seen from Indonesia. It starts from the 87 Indonesian journals in SCImago's 2025 Law list and follows their citations, in both directions, out to journals in Indonesia and abroad, using Scopus's own records.
Read more: goal, purpose, why Indonesia, why Scopus
Goal
An attempt to make sense of an overlooked side of the globalization of law, whether it is happening at all. It shows the citation surface of these journals as it appears in the data: where their citations come from, where their references go, and how the journals connect to each other and to journals abroad.
Purpose
It is the data companion to a study of Scopus-indexed law journals in Indonesia. It lets readers check each figure in the study, see how it was computed, and look up any single journal.
Why this reading matters
Law has long travelled with the people who write about it: judges and scholars carry their books and habits with them. If legal scholarship is a dialogue in which everyone may take part on an equal footing, citations are one place where we can check whether it is. Studies of international criminal courts have found that they cite Western law far more than the law of the countries whose cases they hear. This page asks the same question of scholarship: now that Indonesian law journals sit in the global index, does the conversation run both ways?
Why Indonesia
Indonesia is here more a starting point to understand a much bigger happening. Indonesia has 87 law journals in SCImago's 2025 Law list, 22 of them in the top tier (Q1). Since 2026, Indonesia's rules for promoting lecturers define a "reputable international journal" as one indexed in Scopus with an SJR above 0.1, and the national publication incentive pays only for articles in Q1 and Q2 journals. Indonesia is therefore a place where law scholarship has entered the Scopus system quickly and at scale. That makes it a good place to ask whether being indexed internationally also means being read and cited internationally.
Why Scopus
Scopus is the database that the measures above rely on. THE draws about 40% of its world university ranking from Scopus data (Elsevier's figure), QS uses it for 25% (citations per faculty and international research network), and SCImago's journal quartiles (Q1–Q4) are computed from it. Scopus also records, for each citation, who cites and who is cited, so both directions can be traced. Both rankings once used Clarivate's Web of Science (then Thomson Reuters) and moved to Scopus: QS after 2007, THE in 2015. THE gave as its reason “Scopus’ wider reach”, meaning more journals and more papers in languages other than English. Its coverage is not all of legal scholarship: books, national journals outside Scopus and laws are only partly visible.
Web of Science, for comparison
Web of Science, the older of the two indexes, is used here only as a second point of reference, to put the Scopus figures in context. The analysis itself rests on Scopus, because it is the index that the rankings and Indonesia's own rules count.
Rationale
This overlooked side is also important, for the globalization of law is carried by scholarly communication or exchange. This is a phenomenon that most, if not all, scholarly work takes for granted. Rankings, promotion rules and funding in Indonesia lean on Scopus numbers, but the numbers alone do not say who is doing the citing. Laying out both directions of citation makes that visible. The page describes patterns; it does not judge any journal or explain why the patterns exist.

    Why Indonesia, and not others?

    Many countries pay or promote scholars for indexed publications, yet few have produced law journals of their own in Scopus. Indonesia appears to combine two things the others mostly do not. Its rules reward a Scopus article at any quartile, so a new journal counts from its first indexed issue, and ministries and universities pay journals to get into Scopus. Only 9 of the 87 journals were in Scopus before 2019; 56 entered in 2019–2021, one to two years after lecturers' allowances were tied to publishing (2017–2018). Where rules reward only Web of Science or high impact (China until 2020, Türkiye), or where a national list gives domestic law journals career value without Scopus (China's Chinese-language list, India, Iran), few law journals entered Scopus. Where Scopus status sets a journal's national rank (Ukraine, Colombia, Brazil), many did. These are readings of timing and rules, not proof of cause.

    Who pays to publish: author fees

    Most of the journals are free to read online, and most charge the author. Of the 87, 51 state an article processing charge, payable on acceptance; 23 charge nothing to authors or readers; for 13 we found no clear statement. The median fee is Rp 5 million (about USD 300); the highest are USD 1,000–1,500. Applied to the 1,973 documents the journals published in 2025, the fees add up to roughly Rp 7–9 billion (USD 450,000–570,000) a year. Three journals take about a quarter of that. Ministries and universities, in turn, pay authors for each Scopus article, up to Rp 40 million from the Ministry of Religious Affairs, often more than the fee itself. A plausible reading, not tested here, is that public money pays for much of this market.

    A second index for comparison: Web of Science

    Web of Science barely sees these journals. Of the 87 journals in SCImago's Law list, only 2 are in the Web of Science Core Collection, both in its Emerging Sources Citation Index: Jurnal Ilmiah Peuradeun (468 records) and Indonesia Law Review (291). Indonesia is nonetheless entering this index too. Law publications with an author in Indonesia rose from 88 in 2022 to 201 in 2025, and 2026 has already passed that (202), above the earlier peak of 2018 (190). Two thirds of the 1,733 such publications are in the Emerging Sources index; 153 are in the Social Sciences Citation Index.

    Why Scopus rather than Web of Science? Plausible reasons
    The rankings
    QS moved its citation data from Thomson Reuters (now Clarivate) to Scopus after 2007, and THE did the same in 2015, citing “Scopus’ wider reach”: more journals, and more papers in languages other than English (THE, 2015).
    The incentives
    Indonesia's own scores are built on Scopus. SINTA scores lecturers by Scopus quartile and counts Scopus citations, journal accreditation treats Scopus indexing as equal to SINTA 1, and many university payments are set by Scopus quartile (see the timeline). Where a rule also accepts Web of Science, it asks for a Journal Impact Factor (at least 0.05 under the 2019 promotion guidelines). Journals in the Emerging Sources index, the usual entry point, received impact factors only from 2023 and quartiles only from 2024 (Clarivate, 2023). For an Indonesian journal, a place in Web of Science long brought little that a place in Scopus did not already bring.
    The assessment
    Web of Science journals are judged by Clarivate's in-house editors against 28 criteria. A journal that meets the 24 quality criteria enters the Emerging Sources index; only one that also meets the 4 citation-based impact criteria reaches the Social Sciences Citation Index, and a rejected journal waits at least two years before it may try again (Clarivate, 2019). Scopus titles are judged by an external board of subject experts on five broader areas, among them an English-language homepage and citedness in Scopus itself (Elsevier).
    Keep in mind
    These reasons are inferred from the rules and the indexes' own descriptions. The page does not test them.

    Conference papers and books, in both indexes

    In Scopus, the conversation around these journals runs almost entirely through journals. Of the 21,972 documents that cite them, 433 (2.0%) are conference papers and about 314 (1.4%) are books, chapters or book series. Who writes them differs by type. Of the 307 conference papers Scopus could match by DOI, 223 have an author in Indonesia, led by Universitas Muhammadiyah Yogyakarta (37), Binus (16) and Diponegoro (14). The books and chapters come mostly from elsewhere: of 237, only 43 have an author in Indonesia, fewer than India (54), with Malaysia (31) third. By name, about 43% of the conference papers and 29% of the books have an author who has also published in the listed journals. In Web of Science the picture is different. Of the 1,733 law publications with an author in Indonesia, 273 (16%) are conference papers and 156 (9%) are books or chapters, many of them proceedings of conferences held in Indonesia, such as the International Conference on Indonesian Legal Studies (2018, 2019), and 64% of them published by Atlantis Press. The books run the other way: Routledge (72) and Cambridge University Press (33) publish two thirds of them, and co-authors abroad are common (Australia 18, the US 11). The two listed journals that Web of Science covers are cited mostly by journals: 18 of their 831 citing documents are conference papers and 18 are books.

    What Web of Science does not see

    Because 85 of the 87 journals are not indexed in Web of Science, the conversation they carry is mostly invisible there. Take seven of the most cited (Samarah, El-Usrah, Ijtihad, Al-Ihkam, Al-Manahij, Volksgeist and El-Mashlahah). Scopus records 6,036 documents citing them in their latest four-year windows. Web of Science finds 538 documents that cite them, across 2018–2026, and those citations count for no journal, since the cited journals have no record there. Two thirds of the 538 come from Indonesia and a fifth from Malaysia; the largest single source is Jurnal Ilmiah Peuradeun, one of the two listed journals that Web of Science does cover (116). Seen from Web of Science, Indonesian law scholarship appears through a few doors: two journals, conference proceedings, and foreign journals that Indonesian authors publish in.

    Method and data

    The page reads citations as a surface: a record of who mentions whom, taken as it stands. It counts that surface in two directions, compares it with other journals and countries, and leaves the reasons behind it open.

    The data
    The journals
    The 87 Indonesian journals in SCImago's 2025 Law list (subject category 3308), with their quartiles. 85 are analyzed in full: one has no Scopus export and one is outside the open-access scope.
    Incoming citations
    Scopus's "citing documents" export for each journal, covering its latest CiteScore window: 21,972 citing rows in all. Each row is one document that cites the journal, with its source title, year and DOI.
    Outgoing references
    Scopus's export of the 7,046 documents the journals published in 2022–2025, with their reference lists: about 290,000 references.
    Publishers and subjects
    Scopus's own source list (October 2026), which gives each indexed journal's publisher and subject codes.
    Countries
    SCImago's 2025 Law rankings, read country by country (72 countries so far), for the number of law journals and their quartiles.
    Editorial boards
    Board lists from the journals' own web pages (70 journals), used only to count shared members between pairs of journals.
    Abstracts and headings
    Up to 40 English abstracts per journal from 2022–2025 (2,963 in all), chosen in a fixed pseudo-random order from Crossref and matched to Scopus, plus section headings of 89 full texts. The comparison set is 199 abstracts and 18 full texts from ten journals of Cambridge, Oxford and other university presses.
    Author fees
    The fee page of each of the 87 journals, read on 15 November 2026, with DOAJ records where a site could not be reached; article counts per journal from SCImago 2025. "No fee" means free to read and free to publish (often called diamond open access).
    Web of Science (comparison only)
    Searches of the Web of Science Core Collection, all editions, made through a university subscription on 15 November 2026: the 87 journals by ISSN, and all law publications (Web of Science category Law) with an author address in Indonesia.
    Rules
    The timeline uses the published texts of ministry regulations and decrees, quoted from the documents themselves.
    How the analysis works
    Two directions
    Every journal is looked at twice: as a receiver (who cites it) and as a giver (whom it cites). Most of the page sets the two side by side, because one direction alone says little about a dialogue.
    Placing a venue
    Each citing or cited venue is placed in a group (listed journal, other Indonesian venue, Global South, Northern journal, and so on) by its title, DOI prefix and Scopus record. Where these give no answer, the venue is left as "unclear" and not guessed.
    Counting
    The unit is a citation: one document mentioning one journal. Self-citations are left out of the exchanges between journals. Shares are taken of all rows that could be placed, and the counts behind each share are shown on hover.
    Comparing
    Indonesian figures are set against a yardstick: other countries in the same SCImago list, journals of Western university presses, or the same journals in an earlier year. A number is read only beside its comparison.
    Reading texts
    Template features are found by matching word patterns in abstracts and headings ("this study aims to", "the results show", "normative juridical"), the same way for both sets of journals.
    How to interpret the figures
    What a citation shows
    A citation is a trace that one text was mentioned in another. It does not measure quality, influence or agreement, and it does not show who read a work without citing it.
    Patterns, not motives
    The page describes how citations are distributed. It does not say why. Words such as "net receiver" and "net giver" describe a balance, and are not a verdict on any journal.
    Asymmetry
    When the journals cite a group often and the group rarely cites them back, the page calls the exchange asymmetric, or one-way. This is a fact about the two counts, read together.
    What Scopus cannot see
    Books, laws, court decisions and journals outside Scopus are only partly visible, so the page shows the indexed part of legal scholarship, the part that rankings and promotion rules count.
    Approximate numbers
    Venue country, reference language and template features are classified by rules, not read by hand, so small differences should not be pressed. The large gaps (for example 6,868 citations to university-press journals against 14 back) are well beyond what such errors could produce.

    SCImago assigns a journal to the country of its publisher, so the large hubs (the United States, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands) include journals edited elsewhere. The country comparison therefore sets them apart.

    Indicators and formulas

    Show the indicators and formulas
    Citing rowsOne row per document in Scopus that cites the journal within the journal's latest CiteScore window (the "citing documents" export).
    % from Indonesian venues(citing rows from Indonesian venues) ÷ (all citing rows) × 100. Indonesian venues are the listed law journals plus other Indonesian journals and proceedings, identified by title and DOI prefix.
    Received (in the list)For journal A: number of citing rows in A's export whose source is another listed journal. A journal citing itself is not counted.
    Given (in the list)For journal A: number of times A appears as the citing source in the other listed journals' exports.
    NetReceived − Given. Above zero: a net receiver. Below zero: a net giver.
    Received ÷ givenReceived ÷ Given. 1 means balance; 2 means a journal receives twice what it gives.
    Venue share(citing rows from a venue group) ÷ (21,972 citing rows across 85 journals) × 100. Venue country is classified from DOI prefix and title keywords, so it is approximate.
    Network linkA line from venue B to journal A carries number of citing rows in A's export whose source is B. Self-citations are left out. Lines from outside groups count the rows in each venue group (see Venue share).
    References (outgoing)Every reference in Scopus's export of the 7,046 documents published by the 87 journals in 2022–2025. The cited venue is read from each reference string and matched by title, so the split between Indonesian and foreign periodicals is approximate.
    Connecting journalAn outside journal with citations it gives to listed journals ≥ 10 and citations it receives from listed journals ≥ 10. Giving is taken from the citing-documents exports; receiving from the reference lists.
    Publisher and subjectEach cited or citing journal is matched by title to Scopus's source list (October 2026). "Western university press" means presses such as Oxford, Cambridge, Chicago, Johns Hopkins, Duke, Edinburgh, Toronto and MIT; "major Western commercial publisher" means Elsevier, Springer, Wiley, Taylor & Francis, SAGE, Emerald, Brill and De Gruyter. A journal counts as law if Law (ASJC 3308) is among its subject codes; it counts as a distant field only if none of its codes is in the social sciences, humanities, business, economics, psychology or multidisciplinary areas.
